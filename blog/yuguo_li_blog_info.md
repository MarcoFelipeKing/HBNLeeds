# Beyond Quanta: Professor Yuguo Li Challenges How We Think About Airborne Infection Transmission

How do we explain superspreading events? Why do influenza and COVID-19 behave so differently if both are primarily transmitted through the air? And can we model infection risk across an entire city without losing the complexity of what happens inside individual buildings?

These were among the questions explored by Professor Yuguo Li (The University of Hong Kong) during a thought-provoking seminar co-hosted by the Healthy Buildings Network and the Leeds Institute for Fluid Dynamics at the University of Leeds this week. The seminar formed part of our ongoing series bringing together researchers from across engineering, public health, architecture, environmental science, epidemiology and the built environment to tackle some of society's most pressing challenges.

Rather than presenting an incremental improvement to existing models, Professor Li proposed something more ambitious: a rethinking of the theoretical foundations used to model airborne infection transmission.

## Moving Beyond the Wells–Riley Paradigm

For decades, the Wells–Riley equation has underpinned much of our understanding of airborne infection risk. While enormously influential, Professor Li argued that traditional quanta-based approaches struggle to explain one of the defining features of respiratory epidemics: superspreading.

Instead, he introduced a particle-based framework that follows infectious particles rather than abstract quanta. This seemingly subtle shift opens the door to modelling infection risk across multiple spatial scales—from close contact between two people, to individual rooms, entire buildings, and ultimately whole cities.

A central message throughout the seminar was that simplicity and realism do not have to be mutually exclusive. By carefully defining the right assumptions, Professor Li demonstrated how highly complex building systems can be represented without becoming computationally intractable.

## A New Way to Think About Indoor Air

One of the seminar's most compelling ideas was the concept of **Clean Air Equivalent (QE)**.

Rather than considering ventilation alone, QE combines all of the mechanisms that remove infectious aerosols—including ventilation, filtration, deposition, viral deactivation, spatial air distribution and time-dependent effects—into a single measure of a space's ability to dilute infection risk.

This unified metric has the potential to simplify how engineers, researchers and policymakers evaluate indoor environments while better reflecting how real buildings actually behave.

Equally intriguing was the introduction of **Close Time Equivalent**, a new way of representing short-range exposure without having to model every detail of people's movements and interactions.

Together, these concepts aim to bridge the gap between sophisticated physics and practical engineering design.

## Explaining Superspreading

Perhaps the most exciting aspect of the seminar was its attempt to answer a question that has challenged infectious disease researchers throughout the COVID-19 pandemic.

If influenza and SARS-CoV-2 are both predominantly airborne, why does COVID-19 generate far more superspreading events?

Professor Li's framework suggests that the answer may lie not simply in ventilation or occupancy, but in the interaction between particle physics, viral biology, human behaviour and building environments.

By linking these mechanisms together within a unified modelling framework, his work seeks to reproduce the observed distributions of secondary infections seen in both influenza and COVID-19—an important benchmark that many existing models struggle to achieve.

## Implications for Healthy Buildings

The seminar also highlighted important implications for building design and public health.

Rather than treating every indoor space equally, Professor Li argued that a relatively small proportion of buildings and rooms may account for a disproportionately large share of transmission risk. Identifying and improving these high-risk environments could offer more effective interventions than applying identical ventilation strategies everywhere.

His work also raises important questions about future building standards. Should guidance focus solely on outdoor air ventilation? Or should it instead account for filtration, air cleaning technologies, occupancy, room volume and other processes that collectively determine infection risk?

These are precisely the kinds of interdisciplinary questions that the Healthy Buildings Network seeks to explore.

## Continuing the Conversation

One of the hallmarks of the seminar was the lively discussion that followed. Questions ranged from modelling connected indoor spaces and naturally ventilated buildings to translating theoretical advances into engineering standards and public health guidance.

While many of the ideas presented remain under active development, the seminar demonstrated the value of challenging established assumptions and bringing together expertise from across disciplines.

As healthy buildings research continues to evolve beyond the lessons of COVID-19, Professor Li's work provides an exciting glimpse of where airborne infection modelling may be heading next.
