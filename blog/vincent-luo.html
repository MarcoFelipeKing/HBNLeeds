Member Spotlight

Designing buildings and cities that keep us well

Professor Zhiwen (Vincent) Luo works across scales that most researchers keep separate — from the airflow in a hospital ward to the heat island above an entire city.

Healthy Buildings Network • 6 July 2026
ZL Welsh School of Architecture, Cardiff University
Home / Blog / Prof. Vincent Luo

Healthy buildings rarely fail for a single reason. Poor air, overheating, and the spread of infection are all connected — and they play out at every scale. Few researchers work fluently across that whole range. Professor Zhiwen (Vincent) Luo, Chair in Architectural and Urban Science at Cardiff's Welsh School of Architecture, is one of them.

One question, two scales

Running through Vincent's research is a deceptively simple question: how do we keep people healthy indoors without spending more energy than we can afford? He pursues it at two scales simultaneously.

Building scale

Finding the sweet spot between ventilation, health benefit and energy cost. Bringing fresh air in is one of our most powerful tools against airborne disease — but heating or cooling that air is expensive. Getting the balance right is a genuine engineering problem.

City scale

A multi-scale modelling platform that connects people, buildings and whole cities to study heatwave resilience. In dense megacities like Hong Kong and London, he has shown how lifting buildings, varying heights and opening wind paths helps cities breathe — cutting both heat stress and pollution exposure.

🌍

Research that shaped WHO guidance

Vincent's work on how ventilation patterns influenced influenza spread in a Hong Kong hospital ward fed directly into the World Health Organization's guidance on natural ventilation for infection control in healthcare settings. Evidence from a model that changed how real buildings are run.

What he's working on now

His Energy, Environment and People group at Cardiff is currently active across a wide front — all of it relevant to anyone working in healthy buildings:

  • AI-driven smart indoor environments — automated systems that optimise air quality and comfort in real time
  • SARS-CoV-2 transmission indoors — quantifying aerosol risk and ventilation effectiveness in different building types
  • Heatwave resilience in South Asia — understanding overheating risk in climates where cooling demand is rising fastest
  • School air quality — how outdoor pollution infiltrates classrooms and what it means for children's health and learning

Why this matters for the network

Vincent's insistence on holding the building and the city in view at the same time is a useful reminder that a healthy building is never a closed box — it sits in a climate, a street and a community, all of which shape the air inside. That's exactly the kind of cross-scale thinking the Healthy Buildings Network is trying to embed.

Members working on ventilation, urban heat or infection control — his group's publications are well worth your time.
