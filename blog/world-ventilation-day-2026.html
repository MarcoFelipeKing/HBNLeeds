Events Five networks 7 November 2026

Act now, or count the cost first?

Five UK networks clashed over legal limits for indoor air. Forty per cent of the room started undecided.

Home / Blog / World Ventilation Day Round Table 2026

To mark World Ventilation Day 2026, five of the UK's leading indoor environment networks abandoned the traditional seminar format for a live, adversarial round table. Across five three-minute provocations and a rapid cross-examination ring, panellists wrestled with a deceptively simple question: should the UK legislate indoor air quality thresholds right now, or would blunt limits without funding, source control and economic teeth miss the mark?

Nobody in a room of sixty building physicists, aerosol scientists, environmental health researchers and housing practitioners is going to lobby for stale air. Yet when a single motion went to the audience at the top of the hour on Friday 6 November, the room split almost down the middle.

“This house believes the UK should set a legally binding indoor air quality limit now, before the evidence is complete.”

Brought together jointly by the Healthy Buildings Network at the University of Leeds, HESTIA, Air Hub, BreatHE IN and the UK Indoor Environments Group, the lunchtime session dispensed with passive keynotes. Five champions were given strictly three minutes and a single slide to stake out a claim and name one concrete policy ask, before facing a sixty-second cross-examination from their fellow panellists. Panellists were asked to take on sharp, provocative briefs, not necessarily reflecting their personal or institutional views, to test where the real fault lines lie.

Round one: five networks, five provocations

HESTIA · for the motion

The net zero blind spot

Doug Booker

Opening for the motion, Doug Booker argued that waiting for complete scientific certainty is a luxury the UK forfeited the moment it began sealing up its housing stock. With homes accounting for roughly 20% of UK greenhouse gas emissions, retrofit programmes are rightly racing to improve airtightness and cut heat loss.

“Airtight retrofit without verified ventilation is a health intervention that nobody consented to.”

Well-executed retrofits lower bills, reduce carbon and keep homes warmer. Poorly planned airtightness, without purpose-provided and verified ventilation, traps moisture, pollutants and summer heat indoors, worsening damp, mould and health inequalities. Invoking the principle of informed consent from medical ethics, Booker noted that residents receiving airtightness upgrades are rarely warned of the respiratory or thermal risks if ventilation fails.

The ask: no public retrofit funding without mandatory post-occupancy indoor air quality checks.

Air Hub · against the motion

Beyond “crack a window”

Dr Abigail Hathway, University of Sheffield

Taking the counter-position, Dr Abigail Hathway challenged the post-pandemic reflex to treat outdoor air as a universal cure-all.

“In our concern about our indoor sources, we seem to have forgotten why we moved indoors as a species in the first place, which is to protect ourselves from outside threats.”

Pointing to the previous summer's wildfires, nearly 5,000 excess weather-related deaths from extreme heat and cold, and the coroner's landmark finding linking Ella Kissi-Debrah's death to traffic pollution infiltrating from outside her home, Hathway warned that simplistic messaging can create new hazards. Air Hub's recent summer monitoring found UK bedrooms averaging nearly 28°C overnight during heatwaves, with CO2 traces suggesting occupants were keeping windows open during the hottest hours of the day.

Rather than a one-size-fits-all mandate, Hathway argued for engineering precision: dedicated point-source extract in kitchens, humidity-controlled extract where laundry is dried indoors, filtration where outdoor air is compromised, and fresh-air dilution in high-occupancy rooms such as classrooms.

The ask: prioritise bespoke source control and targeted filtration, tailored to the building, its occupants and its outdoor environment.

BreatHE IN · for the motion

Compliance theatre in the classroom

Dr Bruño Fraga, University of Birmingham

Dr Bruño Fraga turned the spotlight onto Britain's schools. Following the rollout of some 300,000 CO2 monitors across classrooms, complete with green, amber and red traffic-light alerts, 95% of recommended actions still boil down to opening a window, clashing directly with winter thermal comfort.

“CO2 monitors have become theatre: measurement with no duty to act.”

For Fraga the danger is not merely physical but pedagogical. Flashing a red warning in front of children and teachers without giving them the means, guidance or authority to fix the air sends a corrosive mixed message. “We are all aware of fire risks,” he noted. “If there is a fire, we know there is a drill, there is a fire warden, there is a series of actions that we take very seriously and enact. But in this case, there are no clear actions and no responsibilities.” In an era when young people are already saturated with warnings of impending crisis, alerting pupils to an invisible threat while offering no remedy breeds either anxiety or fatalistic indifference.

The ask: every sensor paired with a named owner empowered to act or escalate, and a mandatory action threshold.

UKIEG · for the motion

The perfect as the enemy of the legal standard

Professor Marcella Ucci, UCL

Professor Marcella Ucci pointed the finger squarely at the academic community itself. Why does the UK still lack a legally binding indoor air quality standard for existing dwellings? In part, she argued, because researchers keep demanding more granular evidence before agreeing on a number.

Citing the Royal College of Physicians' report A Breath of Fresh Air, Ucci noted the paradox at the heart of UK public health policy: air pollution is estimated to cause around 30,000 deaths a year in the UK, and citizens spend 80% to 90% of their lives indoors, yet the report could not put a headline figure on the disease burden specifically attributable to indoor air. Scientists remain locked in debate over whether outdoor dose-response relationships can be translated indoors.

“Whilst we debate about whether the evidence is translatable and what is a good enough minimum limit, we are in effect accepting that high levels are okay whilst we wait.”

The ask: the UK indoor environment community agrees pragmatic interim limits by World Ventilation Day 2027, with a mechanism to review them as evidence evolves.

Healthy Buildings Network · against the motion

Why litres per second lose every budget fight

Dr Irene Mussio, University of Leeds

Closing round one, behavioural and health economist Dr Irene Mussio diagnosed why indoor air quality repeatedly stalls at the Treasury and the boardroom door.

“Ventilation loses every budget fight because we report it in litres per second rather than in terms of illness prevented and cost avoided.”

Airflow rates are indispensable to engineers, but hospital trusts, school estates and social housing providers operate under fierce opportunity costs. Decision-makers do not fund cubic metres of air. They fund reductions in NHS expenditure, fewer days lost to sickness absence, higher productivity, improved pupil cognition and narrower health inequalities, particularly given that the households exposed to the worst indoor air have the fewest resources to fix it. Without translating engineering performance into health-economic value, legal limits risk remaining unfunded aspirations.

The ask: embed a health economist, and proportionate economic and behavioural evaluation, into ventilation projects from the design stage.

Round two: the gloves come off, politely

The running order was reversed, and each speaker had 60 seconds to put a single pointed challenge to a fellow panellist, followed by a two-minute defence.

What happens when a low-income home fails the check?

Irene Mussio pressed Doug Booker on the equity trap of post-occupancy monitoring. If checks happen after retrofit, occupants have already been exposed, and what guarantees that a failed test leads to a funded remedy rather than leaving a household staring at a bad reading they cannot afford to fix? Booker conceded the point and sharpened his ask on the spot: public retrofit funding must tie post-occupancy checks to an enforceable line of responsibility and ring-fenced remediation, so failed homes are fixed rather than abandoned.

How do we advise a country of naturally ventilated buildings?

Booker challenged Abigail Hathway: gaseous pollutants such as NO2 and VOCs are hard to filter, and most UK homes and schools rely on natural ventilation. How do we help people manage moisture, infection and summer heat without telling them to stop opening windows? Hathway clarified that the goal is not sealing windows shut but ending simplistic slogans that ignore building physics. When outdoor temperatures top 30°C or traffic plumes peak, unguided window opening backfires. What is needed is public literacy paired with accessible technology that tells occupants when outdoor air will cool or clean their home, and when it will do the opposite.

What if the named owner has no budget and a Victorian classroom?

Hathway turned the screws on Bruño Fraga: if a school appoints a named owner for every CO2 sensor, what happens when that person has neither the seniority, the estates budget nor the building-physics feasibility to fix the room? Fraga acknowledged that naming an owner shifts the bottleneck rather than dissolving it. But right now the burden stops at a blinking box on a teacher's desk, creating a false sense of security. A formal chain of responsibility, backed by evidence that small ventilation investments pay for themselves through reduced respiratory absence, moves accountability up the institutional ladder.

Half-friendly fire: is research consensus really the bottleneck?

In the most electric exchange of the hour, Fraga aimed what he called “half-friendly fire” at fellow pro-motion panellist Marcella Ucci. Having watched UK researchers spend the six years since 2020 synthesising evidence and briefing policymakers, he questioned whether scientists are really to blame for regulatory inaction. Ucci stood her ground. Chronic underfunding of indoor exposure science means researchers still cannot hand ministers a single indoor mortality figure equivalent to the 30,000 outdoor deaths, and that gap matters politically. “Yes, absolutely, politicians do not wait for researchers,” she replied, “but they will absolutely pounce on any doubt when the alternative is do nothing, which is a lot more convenient at the moment.”

Why would a private developer pay for population health economics?

Ucci pressed Irene Mussio: why would a commercial developer or private landlord pay for a health economist whose models measure population welfare rather than the developer's own bottom line? Mussio drew a distinction between private balance sheets and public investment. Not every contractor needs an economist on payroll; smaller projects can draw on open toolkits and shared evidence. But wherever public money is spent on housing, schools or hospitals, or where building standards are set, proportionate economic evaluation embedded at the design stage is the only language that shifts Treasury and ministerial minds.

From the floor: heatwaves, Lüften, and the glacial pace

Dr Giorgos Petrou probed the behavioural puzzle in Hathway's heatwave data: why do people open windows when outdoor air is warmer than indoor air? Hathway pointed to thermal physiology and habit. Even warm air moving across the skin creates an immediate cooling breeze, making occupants feel transiently more comfortable in the afternoon while silently loading thermal mass that bakes the bedroom at 28°C overnight. Studies also show that people adjust windows when they first enter a room, then rarely touch them again for hours.

Professor Catherine Noakes then posed the strategic question hanging over the sector: given that progress towards binding targets often feels glacial, what specific piece of evidence should researchers prioritise right now to unlock real change?

  • Damp, mould and Awaab's Law. Marcella Ucci singled out the dose-response relationship for damp and mould. Risk assessment currently relies on a binary test: visible mould, yes or no. “The problem with having such a broad definition of risk is that then you need to prioritise everything, which then means nothing is a priority any more.” Even with the welcome introduction of Awaab's Law, better quantitative thresholds are needed so housing providers can triage the highest-risk homes first.

  • Ventilation culture and return on investment. Doug Booker added two missing ingredients. Behaviour is shaped by culture, not just individual choice: unlike Germany's ingrained practice of Lüften, the UK has no cultural playbook for ventilating and shading homes. And, echoing Mussio, the conversations that actually move budget-holders are those linking indoor air directly to healthcare costs and return on investment.

The verdict: did minds change?

With sixty seconds left before the hour struck, the closing poll went live. The Q&A had run right to the chime, so only 16 people caught the closing link before the lunchtime dash. Among those who did, the undecided bloc broke in both directions.

Yes climbed six points, No rose nine, and Undecided shrank by fifteen. The debate had done its job: it forced a room of nuance-loving specialists off the fence, and it did so in both directions, which says something about how persuasive both the regulatory and the engineering and economic cases were.
