# Designing buildings and cities that keep us well: the work of Professor Vincent Luo

Healthy buildings rarely fail for a single reason. Poor air, overheating, and the spread of infection are all connected, and they play out at every scale — from the corner of a hospital ward to the street canyons of a dense city. Few researchers work fluently across that whole range. Professor Zhiwen (Vincent) Luo is one of them.

Vincent is Professor and Chair in Architectural and Urban Science at the Welsh School of Architecture, Cardiff University, where he leads the Energy, Environment and People (EEP) research group. His training reflects the breadth of his work: a first degree in architectural engineering from the Harbin Institute of Technology in China, and a PhD in urban climate from the University of Hong Kong. Before moving to Cardiff he spent a decade at the University of Reading, latterly as an associate professor.

## One question, two scales

Running through Vincent's research is a single, deceptively simple question: how do we keep people healthy indoors without spending more energy than we can afford? He pursues that question at two scales at once.

**At the building scale**, his interest is in finding the sweet spot between health benefit and energy cost through ventilation design. Ventilation is one of our most powerful tools for diluting indoor pollutants and reducing the airborne spread of disease — but it is also expensive in energy terms, especially when we heat or cool the air we bring in. Getting the balance right is a genuine engineering problem, and it sits at the heart of what a healthy building actually is.

**At the urban scale**, Vincent has built a multi-scale modelling platform that connects people, buildings and cities to study heatwave resilience. He has also done extensive work on improving urban ventilation in dense megacities such as Hong Kong and London, where better airflow between buildings can cut both heat stress and exposure to air pollution. His group has proposed design principles — lifting buildings up, varying their heights, opening wind paths — that help dense cities breathe.

## Research that has shaped guidance

Vincent's work has reached beyond the journals. His research into how ventilation patterns influenced the spread of influenza in a Hong Kong hospital ward, and into cost-effective natural ventilation for resource-poor settings, fed into the World Health Organization's guidance on natural ventilation for infection control in healthcare settings. That is the kind of translation the Healthy Buildings Network exists to encourage: evidence that starts in a lab or a model and ends up changing how real buildings are run.

His recent and ongoing projects span heatwave and overheating risk in South Asia, AI-driven smart indoor environment design, SARS-CoV-2 transmission indoors, climate-resilient built environments, and the impact of outdoor pollution on the air children breathe in schools. The funding behind this work — from NERC, EPSRC, AHRC, BBSRC, Innovate UK, the Met Office and others — is a measure of how widely his questions resonate.

Alongside the research, Vincent gives his time to the field. He serves on the board of the International Association for Urban Climate and chairs a scientific and technological committee on environmental and climate impact for the International Society of Indoor Air Quality and Climate (ISIAQ), and he holds editorial roles across building simulation and intelligent buildings journals.

## Why it matters for the network

The threads in Vincent's work — ventilation, indoor air quality, infection risk, heat, and the tension between health and energy — are exactly the threads the Healthy Buildings Network is trying to weave together. His insistence on holding the building and the city in view at the same time is a useful reminder that a healthy building is never really a closed box; it sits in a climate, a street and a community, all of which shape the air inside.

We're delighted to feature his work, and we'd encourage members working on ventilation, urban heat or infection control to seek out his group's publications.
