About the round table

To mark World Ventilation Day, five of the UK's indoor environment networks are coming together for a live online round table. Instead of another set of talks, we're putting one motion to the vote. Each network has three minutes and one slide to make its case, then challenges another network directly. You vote before and after. Will you change your mind?

The motion

This house believes the UK should set a legally binding indoor air quality limit now, before the evidence is complete.

The five networks

This round table is organised jointly. Each network puts up one speaker and makes its own case.

  • Healthy Buildings Network (HBN)

    A cross-disciplinary research and collaboration hub at the University of Leeds, focused on healthier, more sustainable built environments.

  • HESTIA (Home Environment Solutions through Technology and Innovation for All)

    Accelerating the creation of indoor home environments that meet Net Zero targets while promoting physical and mental health and wellbeing for all, considering the interface of technologies and social factors.

  • Air Hub

    Engineering healthy indoor environments, building research capacity to understand what is in particulate matter (PM2.5) inside, what its health impacts are, and what we should do about it.

  • BreatHE IN (Building Healthier Environments Indoors)

    Established to ensure that health is not an afterthought, but a central consideration in the transformation of the UK’s built environment.

  • UK Indoor Environments Group (UKIEG)

    A unique, independent and impartial multidisciplinary network of professionals working in the field of health, wellbeing and indoor environments.

Questions we could not reach on the day

The hour ran out before we got to everything in the chat. We sent the remaining questions to the panellists afterwards. Their answers are below, in their own words.

Asked by Sally

We already have safe limits for each component of indoor air quality, but surely it is about how we understand them in combination? CO2 may be very low because ventilation is great, yet indoor air quality is terrible because outdoor air quality is terrible. Or very high yet pretty good, because filtration is effective. Is one of the embedded myths we need to shatter that ventilation equals clean indoor air?

Dr Abigail Hathway · Air Hub

Yes, we need to stop assuming that bringing in outdoor air will equate to good indoor air quality. But given how little most people think about indoor air pollution, or about ventilating, we do need to be careful that our messaging does not confuse matters. It needs to be recognised that there are times when you do not want to bring the outdoor air in at all, such as extreme pollution events. That message is probably easier to get across, because most people can sense that type of pollution, but they do not notice the everyday sources of pollution inside.

Asked by Stephen

I am astonished that respiratory infections are not figuring more in the discussion. COVID is still here, along with our seasonal viruses. I am also confused as to why opening windows is set against filtration and source control. Why not produce an integrated plan and associated guidance to mitigate different challenges in different settings?

Dr Abigail Hathway · Air Hub

Infection risk is part of the integrated plan, and it is specific to different settings. If you live on your own then the risk of infection at home is pretty low. If you are in a crowded environment for long periods it is high. This type of source is usually unknown in location, so ventilation across the whole space is required.

And yes, in most spaces you would want base ventilation and directed source control. I was being deliberately provocative, as detailed in the brief. Although there are times when the risk from outside is so much higher than inside that we need the ability to be dynamic, which some constantly running fans do not really allow.

Dr Bruño Fraga · BreatHE IN

We do know that CO2 is generally a good predictor for displacement ventilation and for human expiration. It is not, however, a good predictor for airborne or respiratory infection risk. There is an obvious rule of thumb, that better ventilation means less risk and fewer people means less risk, but it is clear that CO2 alone cannot be used to flag infection risk.

There is relevant research being done on finding other proxies for respiratory infection, the bottleneck being their rather small concentrations in the environment. Some interesting ideas revolve around complementing CO2 data with sound measurements, because aerosol exhalation is much more strongly dependent on voice volume, as well as with particulate matter sensors and epidemiological data.

Asked by Gabriela

Are we at risk of creating a system where homes and schools are expected to meet CO2 targets but lack the funding, authority or infrastructure to achieve them? How can we ensure that air quality standards drive meaningful improvements, rather than simply documenting inequalities that schools, homes and occupants themselves cannot fix, leaving people powerless?

Dr Irene Mussio · Healthy Buildings Network

Setting standards without providing the resources to meet them risks reinforcing existing health inequalities. We need targeted funding, clear accountability and economic evaluation to ensure regulation delivers real health improvements, rather than simply documenting disparities.

Professor Marcella Ucci · UKIEG

I agree, standards must go hand in hand with enforcement mechanisms. Not all require funding, but all do require clarity on agency and responsibility, and on improvement mechanisms. This would require co-production with stakeholders, ideally.

Asked by Iza

Are we becoming too reliant on mechanical ventilation with heat recovery as the default solution for airtight buildings? It introduces additional risks around duct hygiene, filtration, commissioning and long-term maintenance. Should we not be assessing which ventilation strategy is most appropriate for the building and its occupants, and then testing actual indoor air quality, rather than assuming a mechanical system guarantees a healthy outcome?

Doug Booker · HESTIA

The 2027 network pledges

All five commitments are in. They sit side by side below, so the community can hold us to them next November.

  • Healthy Buildings Network
    Dr Irene Mussio

    HBN will publish all five commitments, and bring the five networks back together on World Ventilation Day 2027 to report on progress.

  • HESTIA
    Dr. Doug Booker

    HESTIA will outline its co-developed, health-equity-centred engineering approach to home design and retrofit, which aims to create indoor home environments that meet Net Zero targets while promoting physical and mental health and wellbeing for all.

  • Air Hub
    Dr Abigail Hathway

    “We will commit to continue working with those with respiratory diseases in order to understand how they experience their indoor environments, and how they would want to engage with future research.”

  • BreatHE IN
    Personal pledge
    Dr Bruño Fraga

    “I pledge to promote indoor air quality wardens for labs, computer or otherwise, within the School of Engineering among our PhD students. We can provide them with sensors and, of course, define actions and thresholds. Hopefully we can propose to extend it to the College of Engineering and Physical Sciences too.”

    Offered as a personal commitment rather than one on behalf of BreatHE IN.

  • UK Indoor Environments Group
    Proposed
    Professor Marcella Ucci

    “UKIEG will be proposing to help convene an expert consensus workshop with a clear output, for example a UKIEG position on interim levels for the UK by next World Ventilation Day.”

    Offered ahead of UKIEG Committee agreement, so it stands as a proposal until the committee has signed it off.
